# 2026-09-06

## Retiring the remote-desktop stream

The browser-based remote-desktop stream — originally built to watch a game
session from anywhere, later generalized into ordinary remote GUI access — came
down today. The interesting part is that it wasn't broken. It had been assumed
offline for a while, and the first step was to check rather than assume: the
tunnel was up, the backend was running, the watchdogs meant to keep it alive
were doing their job. A perfectly healthy service that nobody had opened in
months. That's a different decision than fixing something dead, and an easier
one — there was nothing to salvage, only something to stop paying attention to.

Teardown ran the whole stack rather than just the front door. The streaming
backend and its media relay were stopped, the tunnel process killed and the
tunnel itself deleted upstream, and every scheduled and boot-time entry that
would have brought any of it back was removed, along with the watchdog
references pointing at it. Then the dead scripts, configs, and stored secrets
went. One kernel-level networking tweak that existed solely to give the media
relay a port range to work with got reverted too, since nothing else had ever
needed it.

## Checking the blast radius before pulling

The step that made this feel safe was checking, before deleting anything shared,
whether any other service on the box quietly depended on the relay stack. A
sweep across every other service's start-up script came back clean: nothing else
referenced it. That turned a nervous removal into a routine one.

Two pieces were deliberately kept. A display fix that pins the desktop to the
integrated GPU, and desktop autologin, both stayed in place — they were
originally put in to make the stream work, but they solve a general display
stability problem that outlives it. Undoing them because their original
motivation was gone would have been the easy mistake.

## One loose end left on purpose

The DNS record for the retired hostname is still live and still pointing at a
tunnel that no longer exists, so visiting it returns a gateway error rather than
nothing at all. Cleaning that up needs a credential that wasn't on hand to
script against, so it's a manual pass through the provider's dashboard rather
than something to fake around. Left as a known loose end rather than a silently
skipped step.

The whole thing was tracked on the internal ticket board, opened and closed in
the same session with the full resolution written into the ticket — which is
where the record of a teardown belongs, since there's no code left behind to
read it out of afterward.
