# 2026-11-12

## Is the income sleeve's NAV tracking badly?

The question was whether the income-ETF sleeve lags VOO and QQQ worse than it should, especially after the recent whipsaw. A daily, price-only NAV rebuilt from the account's order fills (time-weighted, so deposits don't distort it) and compared against both indexes showed the whipsaw did not durably widen the lag. The "catches less of the up days, more of the down days" pattern is mostly a constant per-day drag from distributions leaving the NAV on ex-dividend dates, not a skewed beta. A small real skew from the covered-call upside cap remains, but it is only about 1 to 1.5 standard errors on roughly 144 days. The early swing trades were ruled out as the cause. The conclusion is that NAV evidence alone is no reason to change the sleeve, and that a fair verdict needs total return versus "VOO plus selling shares for income," since NAV alone shows the cost of an income fund and hides the benefit. That total-return check hasn't been run yet.

## A growth-tilted monthly payer: not now

A growth-tilted, monthly-paying ETF was screened against live price history as a possible addition to the income sleeve. A low-yield diversifier would only fit at about 10% of the sleeve to keep the blended yield above its floor, and at the sleeve's current size the dollars are too small to matter. The decision is to not add one now and to revisit when the sleeve is larger and the blended yield has more headroom. Leaning harder on the lowest-yield covered-call fund was ruled out too, since it is also under the floor.

## Dashboard fixes and a ledger backfill

The income dashboard was reconciled against the brokerage: share counts for every holding already matched exactly, and the only stale field was the cash balance. A small remaining value gap turned out to be price-source timing (delayed quotes versus last trade), not bad data.

The 1Y chart's steep ramp at the left edge had a deeper cause than the first fix assumed. The charts space points by count, not by time, and older history is one point per day while recent history is every 5 minutes, so the first four weeks were squeezed into a sliver of the width. Thinning the 1Y view to one point per day, across all five chart views, fixed it, verified with headless-browser screenshots.

The distribution ledger was then backfilled. The brokerage's tools have no payment-history endpoint, so the sources were the fund sponsors' own Rule 19a-1 notices (which parsed cleanly as PDFs) plus the brokerage's dividend-schedule feed, with share counts at each record date taken from the contribution ledger. Two earlier "reconstructed" rows had the right amounts but pay dates off by four days, and one inferred date was off by a day. All are corrected, the notices' return-of-capital estimates are filled in so the adjusted-cost-basis metric has data, and every backfilled row is labeled computed-not-statement. The app only had an add path for that table, so corrections were made directly in the database after taking a copy first.

## Why prices move if buy-and-hold wins

A side thread landed on why prices move at all despite "buy and hold wins": forced or constrained sellers, price versus quality, two sides to every trade, and real but modest evidence of behavior-gap losses. The takeaway for the lab is that the real levers are contribution rate, staying put on red days, costs and taxes, and checking the structure. Short-term calls are not the engine.

## Experimenting with multi-agent teams

A day of experimenting with how several AI agents can work together on lab questions. Each agent got a narrow brief and read-only defaults, and a coordinating session ran them as parallel background subagents and merged their reports. The most useful result was about format: independent first-round pitches followed by a second round where every agent saw all the others' pitches, voted, and pushed back produced real cross-talk, while a single round mostly produced agreement. In the second round, ownership questions got settled by reading code rather than guessing, one agent withdrew a claim when challenged, and a deliberately skeptical agent rated its own case against the consensus at about 4 out of 10. The agents also mostly argued against adding more agents, and the one gap they agreed on was ruled out at current volume. How often to run this kind of session is still undecided, and weekly or on-demand looks like a better fit than daily.

## Backups simplified

A side finding from one of those sessions was that the nightly USB copy had been healthy all along, and the overnight error came from the cloud-upload leg failing to see an archive it had just uploaded, a shared-API-client problem that was already ticketed. The off-site cloud upload (failing most nights on rate limits and a path error) and the daily backup email (the snapshot had outgrown the mail size cap and had been bouncing for two weeks) were both retired. They were disabled rather than deleted so either can return, so backups are now local plus a USB copy. The agent configuration files had never been in the backup set, and now are.

## A bull-vs-bear desk scene

A multi-agent design vote clustered ideas into five concepts and landed 6 to 2 for a maker's-mark stamp set, with the dissent's durability worry folded into the winning pitch as a printed master plus a registration jig and a proof strike. An income-relief wall piece (privacy of the shape plus a monthly chore) and a server-status plate (two of its jobs had just been retired) were ruled out.

The actual design that day was a bull-vs-bear desk scene instead. A pure OpenSCAD version built from hulled spheres read as toy blobs, so it was rebuilt as a hybrid: the animals come from a scripted Blender metaball model so body parts blend like clay, while OpenSCAD keeps the precise oval plinth and the recessed maker's mark and unions everything into one solid. Gotchas worth keeping: a lone metaball's surface sits at about 0.57x its radius; the "socket" at each horn root that looked like a hole in renders turned out to be a concave fillet, confirmed with a geometric containment check; and the underside mark has to be mirrored on the axis that matches how people actually flip the piece over.
