# 2026-11-08

## The lighter sleeve's fit is confirmed

The third version of the lighter sleeve fit on the first try. Line-to-line rib tips plus two short rib bands landed between the first print (fell out) and the second (needed pliers). That validated treating the two failed prints as a bracket instead of guessing again.

## A personalized variant, and a font-printability study

A gift variant of the sleeve was built with a drawn motif and a name on the front, a title up the back, and the Boyo Labs chip logo moved to the base as a small maker's mark. The fit geometry is untouched; only the deboss changes, through a design switch.

The interesting part was how to decide whether a font will print at all. The old floor check judged by the area removed, which failed even good fonts because every sharp glyph corner loses a sliver. It was rebuilt to judge by topology instead: does a stroke break, or does a counter fill in, under a morphological opening and closing. It was also made directional. On a wall printed upright, features across the wall are limited by the nozzle (about 0.8 mm) while features up the wall are limited by the layer height (about 0.4 mm), so the probe is an ellipse rather than a circle.

Six installed fonts were swept, with and without stroke thickening and letter-spacing changes. A title line about 19 mm wide is unprintable in every font on a 0.4 mm nozzle (the best reached 0.50 mm). For calibration, the previous sleeve's printed wordmark measures 0.55 mm. Splitting the phrase across two faces got each part to 0.8 mm or better. The script font that looked most fitting lost both ways, because thickening its hairlines closed its letter gaps; a heavy humanist sans with wider spacing won. The morphology and component counting were written in plain numpy because the system scipy doesn't import under the installed numpy 2.x, and fixing that would have meant touching system packages.

## A printed garden stake for a tight spot

A standard spring stake wouldn't fit between concrete slabs, so a printed stake was designed in OpenSCAD to hold a fiberglass marker rod upright. It's an offset J-hook whose deep leg anchors a few inches out, past a hidden lip in the concrete. The blades are T-section for rigidity in PLA. It prints on its side with no supports, using a teardrop bore and crush ribs for the rod grip. The first render caught a miter-spike bug from `offset(delta)` on the flange, fixed by switching to `offset(r)`. A separate fit-test coupon covers the socket before committing to the full part.
