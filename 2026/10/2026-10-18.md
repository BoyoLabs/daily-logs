# 2026-10-18

## Printed stencils for leatherwork

A new crossover took shape: designing and 3D printing rigid stencils for leather patterns, which joins the lab's design-and-print side with Woodsman Ways leatherwork. A printed edge holds up to tracing with a knife better than cardstock, rivet holes land exactly where the pattern puts them, and a parametric design can be re-rendered for a different item instead of redrawn by hand. Three stencils came out of the day.

## A can coozie pattern, split to fit the bed

The first was a free riveted can coozie pattern supplied as a PDF. The cut line and all 16 rivet holes were pulled straight from the PDF's vector paths rather than traced from an image, and the scale was checked against the PDF's own 1" square. At 146 x 265 mm the template was too tall for the bed, so it was split into four pieces to be plastic-welded back together. Each cut sits at a panel midline or the widest point of the neck, at least 12 mm from any rivet hole, and each piece has an engraved number for assembly order.

## A riveted knife sheath designed from measurements

The second was an original design: a single-fold pouch sheath for a cord-wrapped fixed blade, closed with five leather rivets instead of stitching. The outline grows outward from the measured blade and handle profile. Near the mouth the pocket widens past the blade, because a round handle needs about half its circumference in flat width or the mouth chokes it. The rivets are evenly spaced along the seam and mirrored across the fold. Two layout fixes came out of checking the render: the bottom rivet first landed right on the fold bend, and the change from blade width to handle width left a visible kink in the outline, which was smoothed into one continuous curve.

## A three-piece wallet, decoded from its symbols

The third was a free no-stitch bifold wallet pattern with three pieces. The assumption going in was that the main piece would need splitting, but every piece fit the bed whole, so the main body and back print together on one plate and the card pocket prints on its own. The tricky part was the pattern's "double-sided rivet" symbols. On the main body they turned out to be semi-transparent ghosts of where the back piece's holes land once the layers are stacked. Two of those ghosts have no punch mark of their own, so they became small optional pin-holes instead of guessed rivet holes, leaving that call to whoever cuts the leather.
